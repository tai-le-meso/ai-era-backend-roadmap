What I learn
Latency, throughput, utilisationThree different questions. Latency is per-request time; throughput is requests per second; utilisation is how busy the resource is. Queueing theory ties them: as utilisation approaches 1, queue length — and therefore latency — grows without bound. The practical knee is around 70–80%.
Why the mean liesRequest latency is right-skewed: a few slow requests drag the mean while the median stays flat. p99 is the number your users notice, and at scale a user making 100 requests hits your p99 roughly once per page load.
Coordinated omissionIf your load generator waits for a response before sending the next request, it stops sending during a stall — so the stall never appears in the results. The fix is an open-model generator that sends at a fixed rate regardless of responses. Most naive harnesses get this wrong and report beautiful, false percentiles.
JMH's jobJMH prevents the JIT from deleting your benchmark: Blackhole consumes results, @State controls sharing, forks isolate JVM state, and warmup iterations get you to steady state. Without these, you measure nothing.
What I do
  1. Read JMH samples 01–09 — especially the dead-code-elimination and constant-folding ones.
  2. Write down what coordinated omission would do to your existing measurements.
  3. Check whether your k6 config uses an open or closed model.
  4. List the flagship's 3–5 genuinely hot paths that deserve a JMH benchmark.
What I conclude
  • A benchmark without a Blackhole may measure an empty loop; JMH exists because the JIT is smarter than the benchmark author.
  • Coordinated omission makes bad systems look good — it is the single most common measurement error in load testing.
What I remember & apply
Utilisation past ~70–80% makes latency grow non-linearly → apply: set autoscaling targets below the knee, not at 90% CPU.
Coordinated omission hides stalls → apply: use an open-model load generator with a fixed arrival rate.
JMH needs Blackhole + forks + warmup → apply: any benchmark without them is not evidence.
p99 is the user-visible number → apply: write SLOs against percentiles, never means.
