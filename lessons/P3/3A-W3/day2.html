Learn II → Design — Circuit breakers, bulkheads & load shedding

What I learn
Sizing the shedderMeasure the queue depth or latency at which the service stops meeting its SLO. Shed above it. The threshold must come from measurement — a guessed threshold either sheds healthy traffic or never fires.
Degradation that is actually usefulA fallback returning an empty list where the caller expects data can be worse than an error — it looks like 'no results' rather than 'unavailable'. Fallbacks must be honest: a cached value with a staleness indicator, or a clear error.
Half-open flappingToo few trial calls and one unlucky failure re-opens the breaker; too many and you flood a recovering dependency. Tune with the recovering dependency in mind, not the failing one.
What I do
  1. Implement a load shedder returning 503 with Retry-After above a measured threshold.
  2. Implement honest fallbacks per dependency, per your degradation matrix.
  3. Test each breaker, bulkhead and shedder under injected fault.
  4. Verify the shedder never sheds health checks.
What I conclude
  • A silent fallback that returns wrong-looking-right data is worse than a visible failure.
  • Every threshold in this week's work must trace back to a measurement.
What I remember & apply
Shed threshold must come from measurement → apply: derive from the SLO, not from a round number.
Fallbacks must be honest → apply: cached-with-staleness or explicit error; never a fake empty result.
Exclude health checks from shedding → apply: otherwise the platform kills a service that is correctly protecting itself.
